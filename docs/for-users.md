# If AI is being used on you

For people affected by AI decisions — job applicants, customers, patients, borrowers, claimants,
employees. No organisation required, no jargon.

You have rights here. Most people do not know what they are, which is most of why they go unused.

---

## How to tell you are dealing with AI

From **2 August 2026**, an organisation operating an AI system that interacts with you has to tell
you it is AI. Not in the terms and conditions — in the interaction itself, before it starts.

Signals that you might be talking to a machine:

- Instant, uniformly long replies at any hour
- It cannot deviate from a script or answer a genuinely novel question
- It repeats your words back in slightly rearranged form
- It never says "I don't know" or "let me check"

**Just ask.** "Am I talking to a person or an AI?" An organisation following the rules answers
directly. One that dodges the question is telling you something too.

## How to tell content is AI-generated

From 2 August 2026, AI-generated images, audio, video, and text published to inform you on matters
of public interest have to be disclosed as such. Deepfakes — content resembling real people or
events — have to be labelled at first exposure.

Exemptions exist: art, satire, fiction, and content that went through genuine human editorial
review. The disclosure still has to exist for artistic work, just without spoiling it.

## Your rights

### To know AI is involved in a decision about you

Ask: **"Was AI used in this decision, and how?"**

From **2 December 2027**, if a decision about you was made using a high-risk AI system and it has
legal effects or significantly affects you, you have a right under AI Act Article 86 to a **clear
and meaningful explanation of the role the AI played and the main elements of the decision.**

You can ask before then. Many organisations will answer, and their willingness to is informative.

### To have a human decide — this right you have already

**GDPR Article 22 has applied since 2018.** You have the right not to be subject to a decision based
*solely* on automated processing that produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects you.

Where automated decision-making is allowed (contract necessity, your explicit consent, or a law),
you still have the right to:

- **human intervention** — a person who actually looks at it
- **express your point of view**
- **contest the decision**

"A human clicked approve" is not enough. The person must have the authority and the actual ability
to reach a different conclusion. Rubber-stamping does not take a decision out of Article 22.

### To know where your data went

Under GDPR Articles 13, 14, and 15 you can ask which **recipients** got your data, whether it left
the EU, on what legal basis, and how long it is kept.

This is the question most organisations answer worst. A conversation with an AI assistant usually
means the text went to a large technology company, often on servers outside the EU. That is
frequently lawful — but they have to be able to tell you.

### To a copy of your data, and to correction and erasure

Standard GDPR rights (Articles 15, 16, 17). They apply to what you typed into a chatbot as much as
to anything else.

## How to ask

Short, specific, in writing. Email is fine. You do not need a lawyer or a template service.

> Subject: Data protection request — [your reference]
>
> Hello,
>
> Regarding [application / account / decision] on [date], I would like to know:
>
> 1. Whether AI was used in this decision, and what role it played
> 2. Which companies received my personal data, and in which countries it was processed
> 3. How long my data is kept
> 4. Whether the decision was made solely by automated means
>
> If the decision was automated, I request human review under Article 22 GDPR, and I would like to
> explain my situation.
>
> Please confirm receipt. I understand a response is due within one month.
>
> [Name, contact, any reference number]

**Deadline:** one month, extendable by two more for complex requests — they have to tell you if they
extend, and why.

**Free of charge.** They may charge only for manifestly unfounded or excessive repeat requests.

## If they do not answer, or the answer is inadequate

1. **Follow up in writing**, referencing your original date. Many non-responses are administrative,
   not deliberate.
2. **Complain to your data protection authority.** Free, and you complain in your own country
   regardless of where the company is. In Germany, the authority of your federal state; the
   [EDPB lists all of them](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/about-edpb/about-edpb/members_en).
3. **For AI Act issues**, complain to your national market surveillance authority. Article 85 gives
   you an explicit right to complain.

Complaints are how enforcement actually happens. Authorities act on patterns, and patterns are built
from individual complaints. Yours counts even if the answer to you personally is small.

## What this repository does not give you

**It does not open the black box.** Nobody can currently give you a faithful account of what happens
inside a large AI model — not the company deploying it, and not the company that built it.

What the law gives you is different, and still worth having: the right to know AI was involved, to
know where your data went, to get a meaningful explanation of the AI's role in a decision, and to
have a human decide instead.

Do not accept "the system decided, we can't explain it" as a final answer. That is not a technical
limitation on the questions you are entitled to ask.
