# AI Usage Policy

Template for staff. Serves AI Act Article 4 (AI literacy), Article 5 screening, Article 26(7)
(worker information), and GDPR Article 5(1)(b)(c).

**Write it short.** A twelve-page policy is a policy nobody reads, and an unread policy provides no
protection whatsoever — including none for you when something goes wrong.

---

## [Organisation] AI Usage Policy

**Version:** 1.0 · **Owner:** [NAME, ROLE] · **Approved:** [DATE] · **Next review:** [DATE]

### Why this exists

We use AI. It helps. It also sends data to other companies, produces confident wrong answers, and
falls under rules we have to follow. This says what is fine, what needs care, and what is not
allowed.

Ask [OWNER] if something is not covered here. Asking is always fine.

### Approved tools

| Tool | Approved for | Not for | Account |
|---|---|---|---|
| [e.g. Claude, company plan] | Drafting, summarising, code | Customer personal data | Company SSO |
| [e.g. Copilot in M365] | Internal documents and mail | | Company SSO |
| | | | |

**Use company accounts, never personal ones.** Consumer accounts run on different terms — often
including training on what you type — and they sit outside our contracts, our security controls, and
our legal cover.

Full list: [system register](ai-system-register.md).

### What you must not put into AI tools

Unless the tool is explicitly approved for it:

- Customer or client personal data — names, contact details, case contents
- Employee data — applications, performance, health, salary
- Special category data — health, biometrics, union membership, religion, sexual orientation,
  political opinions
- Credentials, keys, tokens
- Material under NDA or third-party confidentiality
- Unpublished financial results, M&A material, or anything price-sensitive

**Rule of thumb:** if you would not email it to an external supplier without thinking, do not paste
it into an AI tool. That is exactly what you are doing.

### What is never allowed

Regardless of tool or approval:

- Making a final decision about a person by AI alone — hiring, dismissal, pay, credit, access to a
  service
- Any prohibited practice under AI Act Article 5, including **emotion inference about colleagues**
- Passing off AI-generated work as reviewed when it was not
- Building or using AI to score or rank colleagues
- Turning off a disclosure that tells someone they are dealing with AI

### What you must do

**Check the output.** You are responsible for what you send, publish, or act on. "The AI wrote it"
is not a defence, and it will not be treated as one.

**Say when AI was involved** where the recipient would care: customer-facing content, published
material, anything someone might rely on.

**Record consequential outputs.** Where a system uses decision records, do not skip them because the
answer looked fine. That judgement belongs to the reviewer.

**Review properly.** If you are named as the reviewer, actually review. A rubber-stamp approval is
worse than no review, because it creates evidence of oversight that did not happen — and your name
is on it.

**Report problems.** Wrong output that reached a customer, data pasted where it should not have
been, a tool behaving oddly: tell [OWNER]. **Reporting a mistake promptly is never itself a
disciplinary matter.** We cannot fix what we do not hear about, and a culture where people hide AI
mistakes is far more dangerous than the mistakes.

### Training

Everyone using AI at work completes [TRAINING] within [PERIOD] of starting, and a refresher
annually. It covers: what these tools do and do not do, this policy, and how to spot a confident
wrong answer.

*AI Act Article 4 has applied since 2 February 2025. The Digital Omnibus softened it from ensuring a
level of literacy to promoting and supporting it — the duty remains, and the training is worth doing
regardless. From December 2027, Article 26(2) requires anyone overseeing a high-risk system to be
demonstrably competent.*

### Bringing in a new AI tool

Before you use it for work:

1. Tell [OWNER]
2. It goes in the [register](ai-system-register.md) with a [system card](../protocol/examples/)
3. Confirm where the data goes and whether a DPA exists
4. Confirm it is not doing anything prohibited under Article 5
5. Then use it

This takes a day or two, not weeks. Skipping it is what creates shadow AI, and shadow AI is where
the incidents come from.

### Works council and employee representatives

*[Where a works council exists, and always before deploying AI that monitors, evaluates, or allocates
work: consult before deployment, not after. In Germany this is a §87 BetrVG co-determination matter
in most cases. AI Act Article 26(7) requires informing workers and their representatives before
putting a high-risk system into use in the workplace, from December 2027 — but the co-determination
duty exists now, independently.]*

### If something goes wrong

1. Stop using the system for that purpose
2. Tell [OWNER] the same day
3. Do not delete anything — the record is what lets us work out what happened
4. [OWNER] logs it in the [incident log](incident-log.md) and decides on notification

Personal data breaches may need reporting to the supervisory authority within 72 hours. Serious
incidents involving high-risk AI have their own reporting duty under Article 73. Both clocks start
when we find out, so telling us late costs more than the mistake did.

### Questions

[OWNER, contact]. There is no such thing as a question too basic.

## Notes for whoever adapts this

- **Cut, do not add.** Every paragraph you add lowers the odds anyone finishes it.
- Name a real person as owner. "The IT department" is nobody.
- The no-blame reporting line is load-bearing. Remove it and you will stop hearing about incidents,
  not stop having them.
- Consult your works council on the policy itself where one exists — a policy on AI monitoring is
  often itself co-determined.
- Review when the tool list changes, when the law moves, or after any incident.
